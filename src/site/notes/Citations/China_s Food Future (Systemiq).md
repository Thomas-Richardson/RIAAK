# Executive Summary / Abstract
[From Executive Summary]

- Food security has become a strategic priority for Chinese leadership, central to economic stability and national security.
- Beijing is applying the same industrial playbook used in solar and electric vehicles to food and agriculture, aligning policy, capital, and technology.
- By 2030, Chinese import demand is projected to have peaked and begun to fall, especially for feed and animal protein.
- Demand for imported soya beans is projected to fall by 25% by 2030, a reduction of approximately 23.5 million tonnes.
- Long term, China’s prioritization of biomanufacturing and alternative protein technologies is set to transform domestic markets and reshape global food production.
- Producers who adapt early—by diversifying markets, upgrading productivity, and raising deforestation and traceability standards—are best placed to succeed.
- China currently runs a large agricultural commodities import deficit of $124.5 billion, accounting for 60% of global soya bean imports and 24% of beef.
- The Chinese strategic playbook operates through five reinforcing mechanisms: coordinated vision, an entrepreneurial environment, financial support, policy and regulatory support, and induced demand.
- By 2040, alternative proteins are projected to capture 14-16% of domestic beef and seafood markets as fermentation-derived and plant-based proteins reach price parity.
- By 2040, China is projected to become a net exporter of several animal protein categories, including poultry, dairy, eggs, and aquatic products.
- By 2050, alternative proteins are projected to meet 35-55% of demand across animal-protein categories in China.
- A sustained contraction in Chinese demand risks simultaneous falls in price and export revenue for major producers in Argentina, Brazil, and the United States.
- The changing demand may allow Chinese policymakers to raise import standards and require deforestation and conversion-free (DCF) commodities.
- China's industrial approach has the potential to dramatically reduce the cost of fermentation infrastructure globally while introducing direct competition in export markets.
- For new supplier countries, the opportunity requires active management to avoid replicating vulnerabilities associated with dependency on a single large buyer.
