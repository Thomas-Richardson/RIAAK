## Executive Summary

Within the UK alone, agriculture, forestry and land use change account for 12.4% of UK territorial greenhouse gas emissions (DESNZ, 2025). The consumption of food in the UK also gives rise to significant forestry, land use and agriculture (FLAG) emissions overseas, along with emissions in other sectors in the UK. Tackling FLAG emission sources within the food chain is vital to delivering on national and global climate goals and all parts of the food system supply chain will need to play a role in delivering change.

The UK's major food retailers are fundamentally dependent on agricultural and land-based supply chains, meaning FLAG related emissions make up a material share of their overall footprint, primarily within Scope 3 (all indirect GHG emissions in a company's value chain, including their supply chain and customer use and disposal). The newly published UK SRS S2 Climate-related Disclosures expects disclosure of Scope 3 emissions, where material, but offers some flexibility in this such that it is not strictly mandatory (GOV UK, 2026). The reporting landscape is however evolving rapidly towards increasing the range and specificity of mandatory reporting requirements.

Nine of the ten of the UK's large food retailers are signed up to the voluntary Science Based Targets Initiative (SBTi) approach, and within which they are required to set specific targets addressing emissions from FLAG sources within their supply chain (Scope 3) emissions. For all food retailers with Net Zero targets, whether through SBTi or independent targets, the FLAG elements of their emissions profile are significant and addressing these cannot be avoided in a credible and robust plan.

UK retailers have taken some steps to leverage change through their value chains but progress remains slow. This project seeks to support the sector in accelerating change by providing a quantitative analysis of retailers' options for cost effective actions to drive emissions reductions along their supply chains.

### Context and aims

Global analyses (Tilt Collective and SYSTEMIQ 2024) identify that the three main areas of action to reduce FLAG emissions are:

1. Shifting to a plant rich food system
2. Improving agricultural production practices
3. Reducing food waste

In the absence of published data on emissions, this analysis has used the total UK food consumption as a route to approximate the Scope 3 FLAG emissions of UK food retailers at a sector level. The analysis has quantified the scale of change that can be expected from retailer driven actions across the value chain. In addition, it has estimated the potential scale of investment required to deliver change. By assessing at a high level, the impact and cost-effectiveness of the three different approaches the work can support retailer strategic decision making in relation to their near term 2030 FLAG targets.

This synthesis of multiple datasets suggests a baseline of 94.85 MtCO2e for food-related FLAG emissions attributable to UK consumption in 2019, which is a valid proxy for UK food retailers Scope 3 FLAG emissions. These FLAG emissions make up 84% of total emissions attributable to UK consumption, the remainder are spread across transport, production, packaging and retail.

### Approach

This analysis shows that the consumption of animal proteins (all meat, dairy, cheese, fish and eggs) accounts for 79% of the Scope 3 FLAG emissions of UK food consumption. The analysis also shows that the farm and feed stages of production account for 61% of Scope 3 FLAG emissions of UK food consumption. This highlights the scope for reducing emissions is strong in tackling both of these routes. Although these figures will vary by retailer, the relative proportions of the different sources are likely to be similar.

### Encouraging Healthy and Sustainable Diets

The dietary change modelling for this report shows that the current consumption of animal proteins makes up around 79% of UK retailers' scope 3 FLAG emissions reflecting both the high methane intensity of ruminant livestock and the embedded land-use change emissions associated with feed crops such as soy and maize.

Setting a target to drive a shift in consumption away from animal proteins to vegetable and alternative proteins would result in a significant drop in UK retailers' scope 3 FLAG emissions due to the lower emissions intensity of these products. UK retailers have greater control over the range of products they stock than the actions of producers in their supply chain. Although shifting the portfolio of products away from animal proteins will require actions that encourage consumers to embrace this shift. Focussing on the health benefits of a diet shift is likely to be an engaging message for consumers. The high level analysis of the impacts of shifting consumption on retailers profits in this report show that a shift in product lines can be done whilst maintaining overall profitability.

Three new diet profiles were modelled for change, along with the WWF Livewell diet. The three alternative diets represented a range of ambition with low, medium and high change scenarios that have been developed to reflect central targets of other work:

1. The 'Current diet' follows that laid out in the WWF report Eating for Net Zero and provides a comparison for change when scaled up to 2030 (WWF UK, 2023b).
2. The LOW change diet matches the UK Climate Change Committee (CCC) Balanced pathway estimated diet change to 2030 (CCC 2025a). Defining features: an 11% drop in meat consumption; a shift in protein sources such that only 64% of protein foods are from livestock; and a shift in dairy consumption such that 88% of dairy is from animal sources, 12% from plant sources.
3. The MEDIUM approximates the CCC maximum ambition for change. In the CCC balanced pathway, this shift is achieved by 2050, though this analysis has explored the impact as if this was achieved by 2030 (CCC 2025a). Defining features: 35% drop in meat consumption; shift in protein sources such that only 50% of protein foods are from livestock; and shift in dairy consumption such that 68% of dairy is from animal sources, 32% from plant sources.
4. The HIGH change diet aligns with the EAT-Lancet Planetary Health diet (Rockström et al. 2025). Defining features: a 67% drop in meat consumption; a shift in protein sources such that only 32% of protein foods are from livestock; and a shift in dairy consumption such that 63% of dairy is from animal sources, 37% from plant sources.
5. The WWF Livewell diet was also modelled for reference (WWF UK, 2023b; WWF UK 2024). Defining features: a 64% drop in meat consumption; a shift in protein sources such that only 40% of protein foods are from livestock; and a very slight shift in dairy consumption such that 97% of dairy is from animal sources, 3% from plant sources.

The modelling of emissions reductions achievable by changing UK diets shows that:

- The LOW level of diet change delivered 3.8 MtCO2e/yr (16% of target for emissions reductions), the MEDIUM diet delivered 14.5 MtCO2e/yr (64.3% of target), and the HIGH level of diet change delivered 28 MtCO2e/yr (exceeded target).
- The estimation of costs suggests that the MEDIUM level of diet shift could actually deliver a positive financial benefit to retailers, given the higher margins on the alternative products, balanced with continued meat sales.
- The HIGH change diet includes a 50% increase in fruit and vegetable consumption, a 30% increase in grains and grain products and a 50% reduction in snacks and desserts. This diet improves overall nutrient intake and significantly increases fibre which is positively associated with reduced risk of heart disease, stroke and type 2 diabetes (BDA, 2025).

The costs to retailers per tonne of abatement vary significantly across the pathways highlighting the need for deeper analysis to ascertain the optimum level of change with minimal cost impact. The abatement in the low diet change is close to being cost neutral, the gains delivered from the medium level are seemingly achievable whilst delivering an increase in retailer revenue, whilst the high level of diet change scenario could result in a retailer cost of abatement of £4/tCO2e. The cost figures remain speculative but demonstrate how the different balance in a shifting diet can deliver win wins for consumers and retailers.

### Supporting Sustainable Agriculture

When looking at scope 3 FLAG emissions by stage of production, rather than products, agricultural emissions make up around 61% of retailers scope 3 FLAG emissions. Improving practices on farms and lowering the emission intensity of UK food production are important routes to delivering a more sustainable food system.

UK retailers do have a strong degree of influence over UK food producers. Most major retailers have invested in building strong relationships with their supply chain and have at their disposal the options to set additional requirements on their providers and using price as a tool for leveraging change. However, the scale of emissions reductions achievable by changing UK agriculture is small relative to the UK retailers' FLAG target challenge.

- Across 42 actions for reducing agricultural emissions the total abatement delivered by 2030 is reported by the CCC to be 2947 ktCO2e/yr (13% of the FLAG target).
- The estimation of costs suggests that this could be delivered with a retailer investment of around £150 Million, producing a retailer cost of abatement close to £50/tCO2e.

### Minimising Food Waste

Emissions associated with wastage and loss make up around 25% of retailers scope 3 FLAG emissions. Minimising food loss and waste at all stages of the food system is a potential win-win action, leading to more efficient business models throughout the food chain whilst also resulting in lower food related emissions.

The contribution of food waste minimisation to the FLAG emissions target challenge depends on the pace of change achievable. Two scenarios were modelled; a high ambition scenario assumes that the UK Courtauld Waste Reduction target to halve food waste is achieved by 2030. This target has the support of most UK food retailers and has been recently rebranded as the Food and Drink Pact. A lower ambition scenario 'Steady Change' assumes food waste reduction continues at a steady pace but the Courtauld target is not realised until 2050. In this Steady Change scenario the reduction in food waste per capita achieved by 2030 is only 34%. The modelling of impact of these shows that:

- Delivering the Steady Change scenario by 2030 would deliver an abatement of 6,332 ktCO2e/yr (28% of the near term FLAG target). The estimation of costs suggests that this could be delivered with a retailer investment of around £50 Million, funding 50% of a small scale campaign, producing a retailer cost per tonne of abatement close to £7.9/tCO2e.
- If the Courtauld Target were achieved by 2030, this would deliver an abatement of 10,942 ktCO2e/yr, or 49% of the near term FLAG target. This is a highly ambitious pace of change.

### Delivering the Near Term FLAG Targets: Key Messages

The three main routes for driving change at a scale sufficient to tackle the UK food retailers' near term FLAG targets have been explored for impact and cost. This shows a route by which retailers could get close to achieving their near term FLAG emissions reduction targets by 2030, if they worked collaboratively as a sector, using leverage to influence consumption patterns and food waste behaviour.

This analysis confirms that no single approach alone is sufficient to deliver on the FLAG emissions target for 2030, and even if the actions delivered as modelled there would still be a shortfall of residual emissions of around 3 MtCO2e. An even more ambitious diet shift could deliver sufficient change to address this gap, but the feasibility of this by 2030 is questionable.

This data analysis has shown that, for UK retailers, taking action to shift consumption patterns to a more plant rich diet is a key route to delivering emissions reductions at the scale required to achieve near term FLAG targets. Firstly, it is the only approach that can deliver significant FLAG emissions reductions, in the required time frame; and secondly, it can be implemented with minimal cost impact on retailers if the right balance of products and prices is achieved. Furthermore, there are additional co-benefits relating to health, food security and mitigation of climate change that mean this approach would deliver wider positive impacts.

Secondly, the results confirm the importance of action to reduce food waste, as this has also emerged as a relatively cost effective measure. Maintaining momentum for cross sectoral action through the Food and Drink Pact (Courtauld) and other agreements should therefore also be a priority.

Improvements in agricultural practices on UK farms are not able to provide the scale of change required to deliver the near term FLAG target, but this does not mean investing in the supply chain is not beneficial. In the longer term these changes can play a more significant role in reducing food system emissions. Furthermore, some agricultural changes will provide environmental co-benefits such as biodiversity improvements on farms that make them worth pursuing beyond the focus of emission reductions.

The UK retail sector has made commendable progress in recognising the importance of Scope 3 FLAG emissions. Yet recognition is not the same as delivery. Current measures — while promising — fall far short of the scale and speed needed to meet 2030 reduction targets. The data shows that for UK food retailers, decarbonisation hinges on systemic changes in food sourcing and diet composition. Reducing sales of animal protein and increasing legumes, pulses and plant-based alternatives delivers the most cost-effective emissions reduction of any FLAG reduction approach.

To close the FLAG gap, retailers should act decisively on five fronts:

1. Set a protein shift target that embeds protein diversification as a central climate strategy which also delivers on health goals;
2. Improve transparency by reporting data on protein sales by product groups;
3. Advocate collectively for policy reform that supports sustainable diets and supply-chain transformation;
4. Support sector wide initiatives to minimise food waste across the value chain;
5. Deepen supplier partnerships to scale regenerative agricultural approaches.

This moment represents both a risk and an opportunity. Retailers that lead on protein transition and transparent FLAG delivery will not only future proof their climate goals but also redefine what it means to be a sustainable, modern food business.
