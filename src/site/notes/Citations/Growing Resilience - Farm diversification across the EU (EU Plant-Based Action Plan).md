## Overview

Diversification is the dormant superpower of EU agriculture.

How diverse is the agricultural production of one farm, one region, one country, or the EU as a whole? Given the European Union's high dependency on protein crop imports and many Member States' low self-sufficiency rates for legumes, vegetables and fruit, one could argue not diverse enough.

More diverse food production systems contribute to many crucial political priorities of the EU, including food security, economic resilience, competitiveness, soil and water management, and the revitalisation of rural areas. For the purposes of this project, diversification is understood as the introduction of new crops (with a focus on legumes), moving into mixed farming and shifting to more plant-rich systems.

Diversification is not just theoretical: many innovative farms across the EU are showing what diversification can truly look like on the ground. Switching to mixed farming, introducing new crops and even diving into on-farm food processing and direct sales, the entrepreneurial spirit of these trailblazing diversification pioneers is inspiring.

This report draws on the experience of 33 farmers from 18 Member States, showcasing key learnings from farmers who are leading the way towards a more resilient food system. Interviews for this report were gathered between January and June 2026 by a large coalition of civil society organisations in the context of the campaign for an EU Action Plan for Plant-Based Foods. The collection of case studies encompasses a wide variety of diversification approaches and farming models, reflecting the rich diversity of agriculture across the EU.

Farmers shared:
- the motivation behind the diversification of their production.
- the impact on their economic situation and quality of life.
- recommendations for policymakers, based on the difficulties they faced.

The feedback from farmers reveals that the European Union and its Member States need to redouble their efforts to develop robust and integrated value chains for all types of local production of plant-based foods, including protein crops. Strengthening local production of these crops will not only offer farmers resilient business models in times of climate uncertainty, but also enhance Europe's strategic autonomy.

The Common Agricultural Policy 2028-2034 and the revised Common Market Organisation Regulation will play a crucial role in supporting the diversification processes already happening on countless farms across the Union, and hopefully inspire many more. Many other EU programmes and instruments could support this promising development: the European Competitiveness Fund, the next Horizon Europe cycle, the EU programme for the Promotion of Agricultural Products (AGRIP) and even the revision of EU directives on public procurement, to name a few.

## Key findings on impact

66.7% of farmers who diversified production report a positive impact on quality of life.

**Economic impact of diversification:** 42.5% of farmers reported a positive economic impact of the diversification of their production - split into somewhat positive (36.4%) and very positive (6.1%). 39.4% of farmers reported no significant impact on their economic situation. 15.2% reported a somewhat negative and 3.0% a very negative impact of the decision to diversify. While the majority of responses are neutral to positive, there is still a lot of economic potential. Many of the farms are still in the investment phase regarding their diversification and hope to see a net economic improvement in the coming years.

**Impact on quality of life:** For 66.7% of farmers, diversifying their production has meant a better quality of life. 27.3% reported a very positive impact and 39.4% reported a somewhat positive impact. 30.3% of farmers reported no significant impact, while only 3% reported a (somewhat) negative impact.

## Policy recommendations from the field

To maximise the potential of diversification, more support is needed.

Farmers openly shared the difficulties that they faced and continue to face when trying to diversify their production. For many, the process has not been easy, so they wish that there was more political and public support for their efforts. What farmers asked for: recognition from policymakers that farmers genuinely want new and innovative products, along with corresponding changes in legislation. They remarked that, at present, the support exists mostly in theory, while in practice shifts in production are still quite complicated and very risky for farmers.

Many of the difficulties diversification pioneers face arise at the level of processing, accessing the market or having to compete with lower-priced imports for consumer demand. Farmers were therefore supportive of comprehensive strategies for the development of plant-based food chains in Europe, from the farm to the final consumer.

The farmer-backed recommendations span across the entire value chain and are grouped into the following categories:

1. Equipping farmers for success: training and advisory services
2. The production gap: addressing yield variability and local processing
3. Financing and derisking diversification: public aids & access to finance
4. A framework that fits: the case for regulatory clarity
5. Securing a market and building regional value chains
6. Further down the value chain: more added value for farmers
7. Helping consumers (re)discover locally grown crops & plant-based foods

All interviewed farmers supported the idea of an EU Action Plan for the development of plant-based food chains, in combination with national strategies for diversification.
