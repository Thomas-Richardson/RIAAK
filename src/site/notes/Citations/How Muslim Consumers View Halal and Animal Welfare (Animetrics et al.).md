# Executive Summary / Abstract
[From Executive Summary]

- Muslim consumers make up roughly a quarter of the world's population and spent an estimated US$1.53 trillion on food and beverages in 2024.
- For many Muslims, halal requirements extend beyond the slaughter method to include how animals are treated throughout their lives, including raising and transport.
- Approximately 63% of surveyed consumers agree that a product cannot be truly halal if animals suffered unnecessarily during their lifetime.
- Around 87% of participants consider at least one common industrial farming practice, such as long-distance transport or confinement housing, to be Islamically unacceptable.
- Most participants indicated they would switch to an alternative product if they learned a halal product involved an Islamically unacceptable practice, though price and convenience are significant factors.
- About 60% of participants, including many who are highly religious, see fully plant-based diets as Islamically acceptable.
- When uncertain about the halal status of a food, consumers turn to certification bodies (61%) and Islamic scholars (54%) rather than social media (11%).
- There is substantial uncertainty regarding the Islamic acceptability of emerging practices like insect-based animal feed, genetic engineering, and cultivated meat.
- Knowledge of stunning is low, with 28.9% of participants having never heard of it and only 28% aware that some stunning methods are reversible.
- While views on stunning are divided, 86% of those who initially rejected it would be more open to reversible stunning if approved by religious authorities.
- Messaging using Islamic framing is more effective at increasing interest in organizational engagement than secular framing.
- Messaging focused on animal welfare is more effective at reducing consumer interest in buying products from industrial animal farming than environmental content.
- There is no single "Muslim consumer" audience; views vary by region, with North African participants prioritizing humane transport while those in South and Southeast Asia focus more on how animals are raised.
