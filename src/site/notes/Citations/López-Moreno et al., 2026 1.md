### Abstract

The 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans (DGA) have been presented as a reset of federal nutrition policy, emphasizing “real food,” nutrient density, and reductions in highly processed foods. This manuscript examines the internal coherence and evidentiary alignment of these Guidelines, with particular attention to how comparator choice and substitution-based frameworks shape the interpretation of food-based recommendations. Drawing on evidence from randomized controlled trials, prospective cohort studies, and global burden of disease assessments, we argue that the estimated health effects of key food groups and nutrients, including red meat, dairy, legumes, saturated fat, and whole grains, are contingent on comparator choice and dietary context. We argue that reliance on individual food characteristics and heterogeneous comparators may obscure meaningful differences in long-term cardiometabolic and population health outcomes.. By integrating comparative and substitution-based evidence, this manuscript proposes a methodological lens for improving transparency, internal consistency, and real-world applicability of dietary guidance. Although grounded in the United States context, these considerations may be relevant to the development and evaluation of dietary guidelines in other national and international policy settings.
