Throughout history, reductions of animal use in the economy have rarely resulted from moral awakening. Instead, just as new inventions have enabled many forms of animal use (e.g., harpoons, battery cages), new technological innovations have also frequently rendered long-entrenched forms of animal exploitation obsolete. This Commentary examines a series of cases – including but not limited to the replacement of whale oil by kerosene, ivory by plastics, goose quills by metal pens, horses by engines, and guano fertilizer by synthetic nitrogen – to illustrate how new inventions, rather than humane sentiment or philosophical arguments, have produced substantial and enduring reductions in animal use. While ethical arguments have driven personal, corporate, and public policy reforms, new technologies’ superior utility, scalability, or cost-efficiency has generally accelerated the abandonment of animal use altogether. Using Peter Singer’s Animal Liberation Now (2023) and its assessment of the limited progress since Animal Liberation (1975) as its starting point, this commentary examines historical cases in which technological substitution reduced institutionalized animal use. The Commentary argues that efforts to reduce animal exploitation should place greater emphasis on developing, scaling, and deploying alternatives that can outperform animal-based products in utility, affordability, or convenience. This approach has implications for how the public and private sectors fund, regulate, and prioritize innovation.
