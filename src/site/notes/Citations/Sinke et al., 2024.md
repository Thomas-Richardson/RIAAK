### Abstract

**Purpose**

Cultivated meat is presented as an alternative to livestock with a lower environmental impact. However, the production process is energy-intensive and recent studies have shown a wide range of results. This study aims to provide an up-to-date and transparent prospective life cycle assessment (LCA) of an industrial-scale cultivated meat production facility that is currently being planned. Its product is used as an ingredient for cat treats, which represents a real market entry.

**Methods**

An attributional cradle-to-gate LCA was performed for a factory with an annual production of 5500 tonnes of cultivated meat. Primary data was collected from the technology provider Meatable for a production process based on pluripotent stem cells. The environmental impact of cultivated meat was calculated for 1 kg of wet mass. The LCA model includes the production of all inputs and the factory operations, including waste treatment. The impact results were compared with conventional meat products.

**Results and discussion**

The global warming potential of cultivated meat is 6.5 kg CO2 eq./kg, which is lower than beef (25–100 kg CO2 eq./kg), similar to pork (4.6–11 kg CO2 eq./kg) and chicken (4.2–6.4 kg CO2 eq./kg). Land use is significantly lower (1.2 m2a/kg) than for all conventional meats (20–200 m2a/kg). The impact results are dominated by the electricity and heat consumption of the factory. Most of the energy is used for the bioreactors, which require strict temperature control and mixing. Other hotspots are the production of the cell culture medium and the wastewater treatment. The results are sensitive to the assumed energy efficiency and the electricity mix. For example, the global warming potential reduces from 6.5 to 2.8 kg CO2 eq./kg if the factory is fully electrified and powered by renewable electricity from wind.

**Conclusions**

This LCA study shows that cultivated meat can have a lower environmental impact than conventional beef in terms of global warming potential and land use. The impact is comparable to pork and chicken. However, the energy consumption of the production process is a critical factor and further improvements in energy efficiency are needed. The choice of energy source is also crucial for the environmental performance. This study is based on a specific production process and the results may not be representative for other technologies. The use of cultivated meat in pet food is a promising market entry strategy, as it may be more accepted by consumers and regulatory bodies.
