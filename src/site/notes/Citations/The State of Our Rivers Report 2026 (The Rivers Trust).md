The Rivers Trust's State of Our Rivers Report 2026 covers the health of rivers across the UK and Ireland. It has **no PDF**. It is an interactive web story (an ArcGIS StoryMap) embedded on the Rivers Trust page, so the only way to read it is online: [report page](https://theriverstrust.org/rivers-report-2026), or the [StoryMap itself](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/4db46e02453a47da897cb3a3ee91f7f3). The foreword (by Robert Macfarlane) is dated September 2026, and the StoryMap was last republished on 13 October 2026. The previous edition was in [2024](https://theriverstrust.org/rivers-report-2024).

This note keeps only the parts that touch animals: farmed animals and their pollution, fish and fish farming, and river wildlife. Everything is paraphrased, with the report's figures kept as stated. The "Flags" section at the end is my own commentary, not the report's.

## The headline numbers (for context)

- England has almost 4,000 river stretches. None are in good or high **overall** health, 23% are poor or bad, and 100% fail the chemical standard.
- Only 15% of English stretches reach good or high **ecological** status, so 85% fall short.
- Other nations (ecological status, 2024 data): Scotland 46% good or better; Wales nearly 60% fail; Ireland 52% fail; Northern Ireland 71% fail.
- The report stresses that statuses use a "one-out, all-out" rule: one failed measure fails the whole stretch. By the Environment Agency's count, 82% of individual ecological measures in England are good or high.

## Farmed animals and river pollution

**How much of the problem is farming?**
- The three biggest reasons English stretches fail the ecological test: agriculture and land management (which includes fertiliser and livestock pollution) 54%, the water industry (treated and untreated sewage, abstraction) 46.5%, and urban and transport run-off 21.7%. The report says these are slightly lower than in the 2022 results but the data is too coarse to say farming is having less impact.
- Agriculture and sewage are named as the two main sources of river pollution. Agricultural pressures listed: animal manures, fertiliser, pesticides and sediment.
- Nitrate pollution has barely changed since the 1980s, and pharmaceuticals in rivers have increased. The report says agriculture is a major source of both. Phosphorus and pesticide levels have fallen since the 1980s, partly through regulation and farm efficiency.

**Phosphorus deep dive (the most useful part for livestock)**
- Sources of phosphorus reaching rivers: treated sewage 47%, agriculture 31%, raw sewage 9%.
- Phosphorus is building up in soils because farms apply more than crops take up. The deep dive says this surplus is mainly driven by livestock and poultry manure, and that intensive livestock production creates hotspots that local farmland cannot use efficiently.
- Some intensive systems make manure reuse impossible because they produce far too much of it. The fix proposed is a move back to mixed farming (crops and livestock together) that does not rely on imported phosphorus in animal feed or mined phosphorus for crops.
- The report says mixed farming cannot match the meat output of intensive farming, so demand will have to fall. It states that eating less meat as a society helps keep phosphorus on the land and out of rivers. This is an explicit demand-reduction message from a mainstream river charity, even though it sits in a short passage.
- Only 20% of the phosphorus applied to crops ends up on people's plates.
- Excess phosphorus feeds algal blooms that use up oxygen and suffocate fish and other aquatic life.

**Other farm-linked points**
- Bathing waters: only 3 of the 22 designated river bathing sites in England and Wales reach the minimum "sufficient" bacteria standard, 12 fail and 6 are not yet assessed. Faecal pollution comes from several sources; the report lists livestock waste and field run-off alongside sewage and road run-off.
- Rivers act as reservoirs for antibiotic-resistant bacteria. The report links this to prescribed (human) antibiotics passing through sewage. The passage does not mention farm antibiotics.
- Pharmaceutical "cocktails" were found at 52 of 54 sites across all ten English National Parks (University of York with the Rivers Trust). Antibiotic levels at some sites could be enough to trigger resistance in river bacteria.
- Neonicotinoid pesticides were found in 85% of English rivers tested, highest in the East of England where they are still allowed in emergencies on sugar beet.
- The chemicals deep dive lists agriculture and aquaculture among the sources of chemical pollution, and notes sewage sludge spread on farmland as a route into crops. The chemicals briefing for MPs lists veterinary medicines among the main chemical sources.
- Fixes the report favours: riverside buffer strips, ponds, wetlands and tree planting on farms. It says river-edge corridors also "prevent livestock contaminating waters". It says regulation alone is rarely enough and that farmers need subsidies and advice, backed by cutting the use of pollutants in the first place.
- Water use: the water-quantity deep dive says a vegetarian diet needs less than 30% of the water of a meat diet. The home-action list says to choose foods with lower water needs, for example more plant-based meals, and to choose organic and regenerative produce. The food and drink sector is named as a major abstraction pressure. Small farm reservoirs to supply crops and livestock are suggested as a flood and food-security benefit.
- **Case study (Making Space for Water briefing):** High Oaks Farm in Cumbria is a 127-hectare family dairy farm with 350 cows. Between 2016 and 2022 it added earth bunding, two sediment ponds and 1,700 metres of riverside buffer strips. The briefing credits the work with a 63% cut in phosphorus across the wider catchment trial, followed by more trout, grey heron and water-crowfoot. Upgraded infrastructure also moved some pasture into rotational grazing and reduced reliance on imported feed. This shows the report's preferred route: fix how farms are run and paid, not how many animals are kept.
- Campaigns the report backs: Making Space for Water (Rivers Trust, National Trust, Woodland Trust and Beaver Trust, asking government to pay farmers fairly for nature-rich river corridors) and Clean Water Now.

## Fish, fish farming and fishing

**Wild Atlantic salmon (salmon deep dive)**
- In December 2023, Atlantic salmon in Great Britain were reclassified on the IUCN Red List from Least Concern to Endangered.
- Scotland, 2025: second-lowest total catch since records began in 1952, and the lowest rod catch ever. Annual catch fell from 111,405 fish in 2010 to 28,020 in 2025 (down 75%). 98% of rod-caught fish were released.
- England and Wales, 2025: declared rod catch of 4,053 salmon, down 33% on the year before and the lowest since 1988. Only two principal salmon rivers beat their egg-laying conservation limit. Every principal salmon river monitored in Wales is "at risk", and just three in England are "not at risk" or "probably not at risk".
- Survival at sea has collapsed. In England only 1 or 2 of every 100 migrating fish now return, against 15 to 20 in the past. Game & Wildlife Conservation Trust research puts the average return at about 3% and often below 1%.
- Ireland and Northern Ireland: about 3,500 returning salmon counted in four key rivers in 2025, 63% below the five-year average. On the River Frome (Dorset), juvenile counts were record lows: 4,593 in 2024 and 3,226 in 2025.
- **Farmed salmon:** the deep dive says DNA from escaped farmed salmon was found in a third of wild salmon tested in Ireland, which could weaken wild populations. No source is given for this in the text.
- Heat: in Cumbria, a stretch with no shade went above 27°C, a level at which young salmon and trout often die. A stretch with about 80% shade peaked at 20°C. Sites with 50 to 70% shade stayed under 22.5°C, the point above which salmon and trout struggle to feed.
- Low summer flows make barriers harder or impossible to pass. Young salmon heading to sea then become easy prey for herons and otters.
- Good news stories: salmon breeding again in the River Don (once declared ecologically dead), record young-salmon density in the Hodder, and a first-ever young salmon recorded in a Derbyshire brook.
- Chalk-stream salmon (southern England and northern France) may be a distinct sub-species (suggested name Salmo salar calcariensis), based on University of Exeter genetics work.
- Introduced species: Pacific (pink) salmon are turning up in UK and Irish rivers and may compete with Atlantic salmon and carry new parasites. Anglers are asked to report sightings and not to return them to the river.

**Other fish points**
- PFOS (a "forever chemical") in freshwater fish averages several times the new EU safe level for freshwater life. Eating one portion a month would exceed the European Food Safety Authority's safe amount over a year (Rivers Trust and Wildlife and Countryside Link).
- Banned PBDE flame retardants were above safe levels in freshwater and marine fish at every site tested.
- Barriers: about 50,000 barriers in England, Scotland and Wales, and about 73,000 potential barriers in Ireland. The report says over 99% of rivers are affected. Fish passes and eel ladders are named as fixes.

## River wildlife

- **Beavers** were hunted to extinction in Britain 400 years ago, the report says for their fur, meat and a scent oil. They are now reintroduced across Scotland, England and Wales, and the report treats them as a nature-based solution: their dams store water, improve water quality and create habitat.
- **Water voles** are being reintroduced. In the 2026 heatwaves they vanished from part of a Yorkshire chalk stream: low flows left banks exposed, so they could not escape predators by diving into underwater burrows.
- **White-clawed crayfish** have declined from pollution and from disease carried by invasive American signal crayfish. Predator-free "Ark" sites are being created. Signal crayfish were brought from North America in the 1970s for farming and the food industry and escaped from farms. Control is by trapping, and they are hard to remove fully.
- **Otters** appear as the example of animals that build up toxic chemicals higher in the food chain (chemicals deep dive).
- **Riverflies** (mayflies, caddisflies, stoneflies) are used by citizen scientists as a pollution warning. In chalk streams, 80% score good on the official invertebrate test while most still fail overall, because a boom in freshwater shrimp can hide the loss of other species.
- **Companion animals:** vet-prescribed flea and tick treatments for dogs and cats leach into rivers. Fipronil was found at 105 sites (37%) and imidacloprid at 34 sites (12%). Fipronil was above the no-effect level every time, and several sites were over 100 times it.
- Restoration success is tracked using electrofishing, tagging of young salmon and sonar counting of adults.

## Flags (my commentary, not the report's)

1. **The 54% farming figure is ambiguous.** The text says 54% of stretches "failed for this" but does not say whether that is a share of all stretches or of failing ones. Check against the Environment Agency's data before quoting it.
2. **The phosphorus source split (47 / 31 / 9) has no stated geography or year.** Do not assume it is England only.
3. **The report disagrees with itself on one number.** The StoryMap's own summary blurb says 22% of English stretches are poor or bad overall; the body says 23%. This note uses the body.
4. **Some claims are uncited in the text:** the "vegetarian diet needs under 30% of the water" line and the escaped-farmed-salmon DNA line. Find the primary sources before using them.
5. **No breakdown by species or system.** The report does not split farm pollution into cattle, pigs, poultry or sheep, so it cannot support factory-farming-specific claims beyond the phosphorus point. For that, use [[Calculating the environmental damage caused by UK factory farms\|Calculating the environmental damage caused by UK factory farms]] and [[Quantifying the Environmental Risks from Pig & Poultry Production in the UK (Cumulus et al.)\|Quantifying the Environmental Risks from Pig & Poultry Production in the UK (Cumulus et al.)]].
6. **The antibiotic-resistance passage is human-only.** It does not mention farm antibiotic use, so it should not be cited for livestock antibiotic resistance.
7. **Fish are treated as stock, not as animals with welfare.** Salmon are framed around conservation and angling. The Missing Salmon Alliance partners include the Angling Trust and Fisheries Management Scotland. Nothing on fish welfare, and the catch-and-release and electrofishing methods are described without comment.
8. **Not peer reviewed.** The figures come from Environment Agency data and Rivers Trust analysis. The interactive maps and charts could not be read as text, so any number that appears only in a chart is missing here.
