# Track it. Sync it. Win it. Racing towards plant-rich retail (Systemiq, Madre Brava, WWF; October 2026)

Final report (52 pages) on how German supermarkets could speed up the shift to plant-rich diets. Authors: Rupert Simons, Paddy Ellen and Pim Doodkorte (Systemiq), Brent Loken (WWF) and Florian Wall (Madre Brava). Landing page, with an English and a German briefing: https://www.systemiq.earth/resource-category/german-supermarkets-plant-based-diets-paper/

# Executive Summary / Abstract

The headline claim: if retailers covering 40% of the German grocery market act together on shared measures and keep competing on price, quality and choice, the tipping point for healthier, more plant-rich diets could arrive in **2030, 13 years earlier** than if retailers act alone (2043). That is a modelling result, not an observation.

## The argument
- Germany is Europe's largest plant-based market (retail sales of €1.7 billion in 2024 across six categories, growing 7.1% a year). The price premium for plant-based products fell from 53% in 2022 to 16% in 2024. Plant-based milk is only 3% dearer than cow's milk, and some products, such as plant-based cream, are already cheaper.
- But Germany still eats the most meat in the EU (about 1,000 g per person per week, against a German Nutrition Society limit of 300 g). About 92% of protein promotions still push meat and other animal products.
- No single retailer is big enough to move the whole market. A retailer that moves alone can lose sales. Example given: Jumbo in the Netherlands dropped meat promotions, lost market share, and reversed the decision.
- At least 25% of the market has to take part before competition between retailers starts to push the change along.
- Retailers act as "choice architects". Price, shelf placement and promotion often shape choices more than information does.

## What retailers are already doing
- Lidl set plant-based protein at 20% of its German protein sales by 2030 and found that equalising prices on its plant-based private label raised sales by 30%.
- Aldi Süd targets 20% more plant-based sales value by 2030 (baseline 2025).
- Rewe stocks Formo's precision-fermentation cheese.
- The WWF methodology for "protein split ratios" (plant vs animal share of protein sales) is becoming a shared way to measure progress. Retailers still read the rules differently, especially for dairy.

## Modelling
- A 2x2 set of scenarios, 2025–2050: retailer participation (40% or 25% of the grocery market) against how quickly rivals copy successful moves (fast or slow). The target is a Planetary Health Diet-aligned split (60% plant-based protein, 55% plant-based dairy). All four scenarios reach it by 2050. They differ only in speed.
- Seven "transition variables" link retailer actions to consumer behaviour: price parity, quality parity, shelf share, assortment ratio, promotion ratio, consumer trial and active consideration. Up to 30 levers are switched on in the best scenario.
- Tipping point year: Systemic Shift (40%, fast follow) 2030; Ambitious Frontrunners (25%) 2039; Scale Without Depth (40%, slow follow) 2040; Fragmented Drift (25%, slow follow) 2043.
- Benefit of Systemic Shift over Fragmented Drift, cumulative to 2050: 426 Mt CO2e avoided (about two-thirds of Germany's annual emissions), 101 million hectare-years of land saved and 13,300 million m³ of water saved. Ambitious Frontrunners: 117 Mt. Scale Without Depth: 86 Mt.
- Perceived quality (taste) improves slowest and is the main bottleneck in every scenario. Faster progress on price, promotion and availability has to make up for it.
- Per the landing page, a two-person German household could save around €1,300 a year on a diet with more plant protein and less meat and dairy.

## Implications and calls
- Retailers: set public targets, report progress openly and use the shared WWF measure. Stay within antitrust law, since the report says it is alignment on direction, not commercial coordination.
- Policy and industry: suppliers need planning certainty to invest ahead of demand, and policymakers should line up fiscal, procurement and regulatory rules with Germany's dietary goals.

## Caveats (the authors' own and mine)
- A comparison of scenarios, not a forecast. The authors say so.
- The baseline is built from retailer-reported data covering about 50% of the market, and reporting retailers are likely ahead of the rest, so the starting point may look too favourable.
- It assumes Germany reaches a flexitarian, Planetary Health Diet-aligned diet by 2050 in every scenario, including the do-little one. That is an ambitious assumption, and it makes the gap between scenarios smaller than if the slow scenario stayed meat-heavy.
- Retail is about 70% of food consumption. Eating out is not modelled.
- Consumer behaviour is simplified, with no allowance for backlash or industry pushback.
- Authors include advocacy organisations, and the "tipping point" timings depend on assumed lever strengths and copy speeds, not data.
