# Executive Summary / Abstract
[AI-derived summary]

*   The expert panel recommends using the Nova Category 4 framework as the scientific basis for identifying ultraprocessed foods (UPFs) in U.S. policy. This is operationalized by identifying "cosmetic additives" (e.g., flavors, colors, emulsifiers) and "non-culinary ingredients" on product labels.
*   The proposed UPF definition directly intersects with alternative proteins and plant-based foods; the report explicitly identifies soy, pea, and whey protein isolates, textured vegetable proteins, hydrolyzed proteins, and modified starches as "non-culinary ingredients" that automatically classify a product as a UPF. 
*   Processed meat is repeatedly highlighted as a specific UPF subcategory with robust evidence linking it to adverse health outcomes, making it a primary target for proposed interventions, such as selective excise taxes.
*   To avoid unintentionally capturing nutritionally beneficial foods, the panel recommends exempting products from UPF policy actions if they meet a modified FDA "Healthy" criteria (containing adequate food group equivalents, remaining below thresholds for added sugar/sodium/saturated fat, and containing no non-sugar sweeteners). 
*   In definition modeling, 21% of tofu products on the market were classified as UPFs (often due to thickeners or coagulants like calcium sulfate), though some could theoretically qualify for the "Healthy" policy exemption.
*   The panel's "Tier 1" (high impact/high feasibility) policy recommendations include implementing selective taxes on high-risk UPFs (like processed meats), restricting the procurement of UPFs in public schools and government facilities, and mandating front-of-package "ultraprocessed" identity labels.
*   "Tier 2" policy recommendations include prohibiting positive health- or nutrient-content claims on UPF packaging. If enacted, this could significantly restrict how alternative proteins and plant-based dairy analogs are marketed if they contain UPF marker ingredients.
*   The report stresses that traditional nutrient-based metrics (focusing on fat, sugar, and sodium) are insufficient for evaluating food healthfulness, as industrial processing alters the "food matrix" (disrupting digestion and satiety) and introduces potentially harmful additives, independent of a product's macronutrient profile.
*   The panel recommends future research into the effects of processing on ingredients to establish a clear demarcation between beneficial food processing and excessive, harmful food matrix degradation—a scientific distinction that will be critical for the alternative protein sector.
