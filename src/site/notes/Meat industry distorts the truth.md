# Meat Industry Distorts the Truth

A synthesis of vault notes on industry-funded science, climate greenwashing, and humanewashing in animal agriculture.

---

## 1. Industry-Funded Research

### The core problem: money shapes conclusions

The pattern of industry funding corrupting research findings is well-documented across multiple areas of animal product science.

**Red meat and cardiovascular health (López-Moreno et al., 2025)** 

A 2025 systematic review of 44 studies examining unprocessed red meat and cardiovascular disease found that 66% of included studies had a link to the red meat industry. The results split starkly along funding lines:

- All independent studies reported either unfavorable (73.3%) or neutral (26.7%) cardiovascular outcomes from red meat consumption.
- All industry-related studies reported either favorable (20.7%) or neutral (79.3%) cardiovascular outcomes.
- Studies with conflicts of interest were nearly **4 times more likely** to report "Favorable/Neutral" outcomes than independent studies (OR 3.75, 95% CI: 1.62–8.67).
- The review concludes that using animal protein (rather than plant protein) as a comparator in industry-funded studies systematically underestimates the cardiovascular benefits of reducing red meat intake.

**Eggs and cholesterol [[Citations/Barnard et al., 2019\|Barnard et al., 2019]]**

A systematic review of 211 articles examining how eggs affect blood cholesterol found that the share of egg-cholesterol studies funded by the egg industry rose from 0% in the 1950s to **60% by 2010–2019**. The divergence in conclusions was striking:

- 93% of non-industry-funded studies reported net increases in cholesterol; 51% of these were statistically significant.
- 86% of industry-funded studies also showed net cholesterol increases — yet **49% of industry-funded studies showed disagreement between their data and their written conclusions**, interpreting cholesterol increases as "no negative impact." Only 13% of non-industry-funded studies did the same.

A representative example: a study giving college students 2 extra eggs per day for 14 weeks found an LDL increase of 15 mg/dL — not statistically significant, but also not ruling out harm. The industry-funded authors concluded there was no negative impact on cholesterol.

A 2025 editorial in the same journal — [[Citations/Khan et al., 2025\|Khan et al., 2025]], "Structural funding bias in red meat research: The elephant in the room" — calls out this pattern explicitly, arguing that the structural dominance of industry funding in red meat research has become the defining methodological problem in the field, not an edge case.

**The systematic note from the vault:**

> "All independent studies reported either unfavorable (73.3%) or neutral (26.7%) cardiovascular outcomes when consuming unprocessed red meat. Conversely, all studies related to the red meat industry reported either favorable (20.7%) or neutral (79.3%) cardiovascular outcomes for red meat intake." — López-Moreno et al., 2025

### The broader food industry pattern

The meat and dairy sector is not alone. Marion Nestle's widely cited 2016 analysis in *JAMA Internal Medicine* — "Corporate Funding of Food and Nutrition Research: Science or Marketing?" — tracked 76 consecutive industry-funded nutrition studies as they appeared in journals. Of those, **70 reported results favourable to the sponsor's interest**. Only 6 did not. Sponsors included companies promoting soft drinks, dairy, eggs, pork, beef, soy, and chocolate. Nestle's conclusion: industry funding of nutrition research functions primarily as marketing, not science. The same paper documents how academic researchers consistently deny that industry money affects their conclusions — a cognitive blind spot that makes the problem especially resistant to self-correction.

Dariush Mozaffarian's 2017 commentary in *JAMA* — "Conflict of Interest and the Role of the Food Industry in Nutrition Research" — reinforces this, noting that poor diet is associated with more deaths and disability worldwide than any other modifiable factor, and that the industry funding shaping nutrition research is therefore not an academic footnote: it is a public health crisis.

### Academic centres captured by the meat industry
[[Citations/Morris et al., 2024\|Morris et al., 2024]]
Morris et al. (2024), published in *Climatic Change*, documents how animal agriculture companies responded to climate evidence in ways that closely mirror the fossil fuel industry's playbook: funding sympathetic academics, establishing front-of-house research centres, and using those centres to obstruct climate policy.

**The study found:**

- The beef industry funded Dr. Frank Mitloehner at UC Davis to critique the 2006 UN "Livestock's Long Shadow" report. Between 2002 and 2021, half of Mitloehner's $5M in research funding came from the meat industry.
- His work was used to claim that cows should not be blamed for climate change.
- The researchers used 20 diagnostic questions to evaluate how industry ties shape research output, public relations, and policy advocacy.

**Two industry-backed centres were profiled:**

- **The CLEAR Center** at UC Davis (est. 2018), directed by Mitloehner.
- **AgNext** at Colorado State University (est. 2020), directed by Dr. Kimberly Stackhouse-Lawson.

Both centres have been involved in promoting industry-led climate "solutions" that maintain production levels rather than reducing them, downplaying livestock's contribution to the climate crisis, and minimising the case for emissions regulation.

> "Similar to fossil fuel companies, US animal agriculture companies responded to evidence that their products cause climate change by minimizing their role in the climate crisis and shaping policymaking in their favor. Here, we show that the industry has done so with the help of university experts." — Morris et al., 2024

**Reference:** https://newrepublic.com/article/179410/academics-meat-industry-climate-davis-colorado

### The funding imbalance in the wider research ecosystem

Governments provide more than **700 times more funding to animal agriculture** than to alternative proteins that could replace them — despite the IPCC, Project Drawdown, EAT-Lancet 2.0, and virtually every major food system assessment concluding that we need to diversify protein sources to meet climate and health goals.

- Global public spending on animal agriculture: approximately $156 billion annually.
- Public R&D funding for alternative proteins (2024): approximately $290 million — about 1.8% of global agricultural R&D spending.
- A decade ago it was essentially zero.

The result is a food system where publicly funded research overwhelmingly reinforces and optimises the existing animal agriculture model, while the science of alternatives remains chronically underfunded. The US alone spends only 2–4% of its agricultural R&D budget on improving climate outcomes through alternative approaches.

*Source: claimingsteaks.com analysis drawing on OECD, Springmann and Freund (2022), and GFI data.*

### US dietary guidelines: political override of scientific consensus

The 2025–2030 US Dietary Guidelines are a case study in how industry influence reaches the highest levels of government food policy. The Dietary Guidelines Advisory Committee (DGAC) made historically strong recommendations for plant-based protein in its scientific report, but the Trump administration rejected more than half:

**What the DGAC recommended (and was rejected):**
- Prioritise plant-based protein over animal protein within the protein foods category.
- Move beans, peas, and lentils from the vegetable group into the protein group.
- Recommend more nutrient-dense plant-based dietary pattern options.
- Found that replacing red and processed meat with plant proteins reduces CVD risk.

**What the final guidelines said instead:**
- Advises consuming double or more the previously recommended protein intake.
- Recommends animal-based protein at every meal.
- 30 of 56 DGAC recommendations were rejected — particularly those supporting plant-forward patterns and cautioning against saturated fats.

This represents a growing divergence between the US and the direction of travel in Europe, Canada, and East Asia, where dietary guidelines are progressively shifting toward less meat.

## 2. Climate Greenwashing

### Scale of the deception

Big Meat and Dairy companies "responsible for outsized greenhouse gas emissions (JBS, Marfrig, Nestlé, Danish Crown, Arla, Danone, Saputo and Fonterra) are making misleading green claims on their products or in their other marketing materials." (Changing Markets Foundation, *Feeding Us Greenwash*, 2023)

The 45 largest meat and dairy companies together emitted an estimated **1.02 billion tonnes CO2eq in 2023/22** — equivalent to the world's 9th highest-emitting nation, and more than Saudi Arabia. The top five emitters (JBS, Marfrig, Tyson, Minerva, Cargill) emitted more combined than Chevron, Shell, or BP. JBS alone accounts for nearly a quarter of all estimated emissions from those 45 companies. (Greenpeace et al., *Roasting the Planet*, 2025)

Despite this, these same companies have reportedly spent hundreds of millions lobbying against meaningful climate action and promoting false solutions.

**Specific greenwashing tactics documented:**

- **"Carbon neutral" and "net zero" claims** lacking credible emissions reduction plans. JBS has been formally reprimanded for this.
- **"Climate controlled" pork** — Danish Crown is facing a lawsuit over this label.
- **Amazon's "Climate Pledge Friendly" range** included beef jerky — one of the most carbon-intensive foods available — because some air had been removed from the packaging.
- **Subtle image-washing**: using pictures of grazing cows on open fields and images of small family farms with happy animals on products from industrial operations.
- **Dairy "carbon neutral" claims** referring only to packaging, ignoring the emissions from the product itself.
- **"Planet-friendly" and "sustainable future"** vague claims that cannot be verified.

The Changing Markets investigation found that in the UK, 42% of consumers are more likely to buy a product with a "carbon neutral" label, and 29% are willing to pay more. These labels are influencing purchasing decisions — without corresponding environmental substance.

**UK supermarkets are complicit** (*Greenwash Grocers*, Feedback Global, 2023): No UK supermarket has set targets to reduce meat and dairy sales, despite these products accounting for around a third of their total emissions. Supermarkets use "selective disclosure" — reporting Scope 1 and 2 emissions (stores and vehicles) while avoiding Scope 3 (sales), which accounts for 95% of their impact.

---

### The grass-fed and regenerative agriculture myths

One of the most persistent climate washing narratives is that grass-fed and regenerative beef is carbon neutral or even carbon positive.

**The "closed carbon loop" myth:**

Some in the livestock industry argue that cattle emissions don't matter because they come from a closed loop — grasses absorb CO2, cattle eat grass, cattle emit methane, and it is all the same carbon. This argument is wrong:

- Grass absorbs CO2. Cows emit methane. These are fundamentally different molecules.
- Methane causes approximately 28–34 times more warming than CO2 over a 100-year period, and 80 times more over 20 years.
- Grass fed cows essentially **convert CO2 in the atmosphere into methane**, which is a far more potent warming agent.

As the vault note puts it: "It's like this: Imagine that I throw a bucket of water at you. That sucks but it doesn't hurt. Now imagine instead I take the same amount of water and freeze it. I now throw a bucket-sized block of ice at you. The amount of water is the same, but it's been converted into something more dangerous."

**Regenerative grazing: overhyped by design:**

Project Drawdown — one of the leading climate solutions organisations — calls regenerative grazing "overhyped." The evidence shows:

- While regenerative grazing may build soil carbon initially, soils reach carbon equilibrium (carbon in equals carbon out) within a few decades. After that, soils stop accumulating carbon — but methane emissions continue for as long as livestock remain.
- The maximum global potential for soil sequestration from grazing would offset only 20–60% of grazing cattle emissions, 4–11% of total livestock emissions, and 0.6–1.6% of total annual GHG emissions.
- Soil carbon sequestration would need to improve by 25–2,000% for any meaningful offsetting — far beyond what evidence suggests is achievable.
- Grass-fed beef requires **50% more land** than grain-fed (and regenerative beef up to 2.5 times as much), making a complete transition entirely infeasible without a massive increase in the cattle population.
- Research consistently finds that rewilding land is far more effective for climate than any form of grazing.

**One 2023 study found grass-fed beef has 20% higher emissions than grain-fed beef** (the opposite of what marketing claims imply).

### GWP*: manipulating the measurement of methane

The meat and dairy industry has been lobbying for adoption of a new metric called **GWP*** to calculate and report methane emissions. GWP* measures the *rate of change* in emissions rather than absolute levels, which could allow companies with large but stable methane emissions to claim climate neutrality.

Problems with this approach:

- GWP* is not valid for estimating company, farm, or country-level emissions. Even small emissions reductions could create a misleading picture of "climate neutrality."
- It penalises developing countries that increase livestock production from a low base, while rewarding rich countries for tiny reductions in large herds.
- **Tyson Foods could achieve a GWP* score of zero while still emitting more than the entire country of Peru.**
- New York State has instead moved to GWP20 — the 20-year global warming potential — to "reflect the severe short-term warming impact methane has."

---

### Biogas: the "renewable energy" distraction

Animal agriculture companies — particularly intensive pig and poultry operations — have promoted biogas digesters as a sustainability solution. The reality is more complicated:

- Biogas captures methane from manure and converts it to usable energy. But it does not eliminate the underlying methane problem from the animals themselves — it only partially addresses manure-related methane.
- One analysis describes it as "creative accounting to dump [emissions] on another industry."
- Because biogas makes manure profitable, it can create a **Jevons paradox**: farms may actually expand their herds because the manure becomes an additional income stream. Iowa dairies with biogas digesters have been documented growing their herds as a result.

## 3. Humanewashing

### What humanewashing is

Humanewashing refers to the use of animal welfare imagery, language, and labels to mislead consumers about the conditions in which animals are raised — giving the impression of high welfare when conditions may remain poor or undefined.

---

### Welfare labels: what consumers believe vs. reality

**Canadian egg labels (Bryant Research / Animal Justice, 2024)**

A survey of 1,006 Canadian residents found that 80–90% of respondents believed that Burnbrae's "Naturegg" and "Nestlaid" packaging indicated good animal welfare. In reality, these labels have no welfare standards attached to them whatsoever.

**US consumer attitudes on humanewashing (Data for Progress / Farm Forward)**

- 69% of American adults are "very concerned" or "somewhat concerned" about where their food comes from.
- Adults' top concerns include companies **misrepresenting their animal welfare practices** (25%), overcharging for products with misleading labels (25%), and inhumane animal welfare practices (19%).
- **62%** support mandatory testing and reporting to ensure products meet their stated standards.
- **87%** support mandatory labelling of antibiotic usage.
- **49%** are already sceptical that "humane" or "ethically raised" labels accurately reflect conditions.
- After being shown more information about food labelling in the meat industry, that scepticism rises to **65%**.
- After seeing a photo of a farm marketed as "Animal Welfare Certified," half of respondents said the operation met their expectations for that label "not very well" or "not at all."

---

### Faunalytics humanewashing study: labels don't mean what shoppers think

Faunalytics (2024) conducted a controlled study of grocery purchasing behaviour and welfare labels, finding:

- Only **9% of participants** knew that "Humanely Raised" is an effectively undefined, meaningless term with no regulatory standards.
- Shoppers associated **similar or even stronger characteristics** with "Humanely Raised" labels than with actual "Certified Humane" labels — meaning meaningless labels can outperform certified ones in consumer perception.
- **44% of shoppers couldn't recall** whether welfare-certified or "Humanely Raised" products had been available to them during an online shopping task. Only 7% correctly remembered.
- Shoppers did not show a clear preference for certified welfare products over unverified welfare claims or products with no welfare claims.
- The availability of "Humanely Raised" products didn't change the total number of animal products purchased — indicating that humanewashing does not reduce animal product consumption, it just shapes which products people buy within that existing level of consumption.

The study concludes that shoppers genuinely cannot distinguish between verified welfare certifications and marketing labels with no standards behind them.

**US shoppers buying on false assumptions (Thibault, Pailler and Freund, 2022)**

A survey of 1,000 American grocery shoppers (*Food Ethics*, 2022) found that 86% had purchased at least one product with a welfare-related label in the past year — including "cage-free", "free-range", "pasture-raised", "natural", "humane", and "grass-fed." Of those buyers:

- **89%** did so because they believed the label indicated higher-welfare production practices.
- **79%** consciously paid more because they thought it meant better-than-standard welfare.

The researchers note that many of these labels either lack uniform standards entirely, or apply to production practices that have no meaningful effect on animal welfare. Consumers are paying welfare premiums for labels that confer no welfare benefit.

**Poultry welfare labels as heuristic shortcuts (Powers et al., 2020)**

A controlled online experiment (n=249) examining five welfare-related labels commonly found on US egg and poultry products found that most consumers lack any knowledge of what the labels mean or what certification standards apply. Instead, they use simplistic label terms like "certified humane" or "cage free" as mental shortcuts — heuristics — to infer humane treatment, without any basis for that inference. Notably, the labels did not lead consumers to pay higher premiums, suggesting that the welfare signal is absorbed psychologically (reassurance) rather than economically (willingness to pay).

---

### The National Cattlemen's Beef Association's PR machine

The US National Cattlemen's Beef Association (NCBA) runs a "Master's of Beef Advocacy" qualification. Insiders have revealed that it functions largely as a training programme in greenwashing and healthwashing — teaching participants how to communicate about beef in ways designed to counter the environmental and health evidence, rather than to engage honestly with it.

*Source: The Guardian, 3 May 2023.*

## 4. The Bigger Picture: Structural Distortion

The distortions described above are not isolated incidents — they reflect a systematic effort by the animal agriculture industry to maintain its social licence to operate in the face of scientific consensus pointing in the other direction.

**The playbook mirrors Big Tobacco and fossil fuels:**

- Fund research that creates doubt about established harms.
- Capture academic institutions and use them to legitimise industry positions.
- Lobby regulators and governments to prevent unfavourable policy.
- Run greenwashing and humanewashing marketing to neutralise consumer concern.
- Promote false technical "solutions" (regenerative grazing, biogas, GWP*) that allow expansion to continue.

The 700:1 funding ratio — governments spending 700 times more on animal agriculture than on alternatives — is the structural foundation that makes all of this possible. It predetermines the research landscape, the academic incentives, and the policy baseline before any individual act of distortion even takes place.
